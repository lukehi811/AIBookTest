CHAPTER 9 — ALEX: "THE SCATTERED ONES"
The world didn't tilt downward; it dissolved.
Alex kept his left hand flat against the lower curve of his ribs, his fingers digging through the torn canvas of his jacket until they found the wet hem of his shirt. Every time his right boot slipped on the slick, gray clay of the ridge, his entire left side flared into a white-hot, grinding heat that made his vision flicker like a dying flashlight. He couldn't check the bandage Emily had tied across his shoulder. He could feel it, though—a heavy, cold weight that had grown stiff as armor in the freezing morning air, gluing the wool of his shirt to the three clean gouges beneath.
He didn't look back at the root ball. The fog had already closed over the fallen birch, erasing it from the slope before they had even covered ten yards.
Ahead of him, Emily was moving by sheer leverage. She had Mara’s left arm draped over her shoulders, her right hand locked around the girl’s waist to keep her from sliding sideways into the briars. Mara wasn't walking so much as letting her legs drag through the mire, her small sneakers leaving two parallel, shallow trenches in the clay. The yellow school sweater was a ruined, gray rag now, its hem soaked through with frozen silt that flapped against her shins with a dull, heavy slap.
Nobody spoke. The rule was a physical weight in their throats.
The descent into the ravine was an exercise in absolute, mechanical silence. The slope was nearly forty-five degrees, a treacherous scar of exposed shale and rotten hemlock roots that had been lubricated by the night’s freezing drizzle. Every step required a three-second calculation. Alex would plant his left heel into the moss, wait to see if the crust would hold his weight, and then shift his center of gravity forward, his teeth grinding together until his jaw joints clicked to keep from letting out a groan.
A sharp, metallic tink echoed from the brush to their right.
Alex froze instantly, his right hand dropping to his thigh, his thumb finding the brass bolster of his pocket knife before his brain had even processed the direction of the sound. Behind him, Emily stopped mid-stride, her body leaning forward at an impossible angle to balance Mara’s dead weight. She didn't drop to her knees; she simply remained motionless, her boots wedged against a slippery root, her eyes wide and bloodshot beneath the wet strands of her hair.
They stayed like that for fifty heartbeats. The only sound was the slow, rhythmic drip-drip of water falling from the high pine needles onto Alex’s bare neck.
It was just a piece of loose shale breaking away from the upper ridge. There was no follow-up click. No multi-jointed appendage sliding through the ferns.
Alex let his breath out through his nose in a long, silent plume of gray vapor. He signaled with two fingers—down—and began the slide again.
The air grew thicker as they hit the floor of the ravine. The light here was different; the uniform white of the upper clearing had been replaced by a heavy, subterranean twilight, choked by massive, ancient hemlocks whose lower branches had died decades ago, leaving a forest of black spikes projecting through the mist. The ground turned from slick clay to a deep, sucking silt—the ancient bed of a creek that had dried up before the breakdown.
With every step, the mud fought to strip their shoes from their feet.
Emily’s strength was visibly fracturing. Her shoulders were hitched high, her breath coming in short, trembling hitches that she forced herself to draw through her nose to muffle the sound. Her jeans were torn at both knees, the skin beneath a raw, purple smear from her slide down the bank the previous night. When Mara’s right boot caught on a buried branch, both girls went down.
There was no cry. Emily simply turned her torso as they fell, taking the brunt of the impact on her uninjured hip, her arms wrapping around Mara’s head to keep the child’s skull from hitting the stones in the silt.
Alex scrambled toward them on his knees, his ribs screaming a protest that nearly turned his stomach inside out. He reached down with his left arm, his fingers hooking into the collar of Emily’s coat to hoist her up. Her face was inches from his—her skin was a translucent gray, her lips cracked and bleeding from where she had bitten them to keep silent.
I can’t carry her, her eyes said, the raw, terrifying admission flashing through the exhaustion. Alex, I can’t carry her anymore.
Alex didn't look away. He took Mara’s limp right hand from Emily’s shoulder and transferred the weight to his own uninjured left side. The girl’s hand was completely unresponsive, her fingers stiff as dry pine needles, her face tucked down into the collar of her sweater so that only the pale, mud-streaked crown of her head was visible. She wasn't fighting them, but she wasn't helping either. She was a fifty-pound anchor in a sea of gray mud.
He braced his boots into the silt, his jaw locking so hard a trickle of copper-tasting saliva ran down his chin. He pulled the girl up against his hip, his left arm locking around her lower back while his right hand held the closed pocket knife against his thigh.
He looked at Emily, nodding once toward the darker shadows of the ravine floor ahead.
The fog down here didn't drift; it hung in heavy, motionless curtains between the black trunks, smelling of wet iron and old rot. They had covered less than a mile since leaving the root ball, but the geometry of the valley had already changed. The high ridges were gone, buried behind the wall of white, leaving them entirely alone in a narrow ditch of stone and mud that seemed to lead nowhere but deeper into the dark.
And then, from thirty yards ahead through the gray lace of the briars, the silence changed.
It wasn't a click. It wasn't the reedy, synthetic voice of a mimic. It was a low, wet, rhythmic scratching sound—the unmistakable cadence of a metal spade digging into frozen earth.
—
The scratching stopped the moment Alex’s boot squelched into a fresh pocket of silt.
Through the gray, lace-like canopy of the briars, the silhouette took shape. It was low, hunched, and entirely human, huddled inside a narrow, hand-dug trench that cut across the belly of the ravine like an open wound.
The man was kneeling in six inches of standing water. His clothes were unrecognizable, caked in so many layers of dried and wet clay that he seemed to melt directly into the walls of his ditch. Around his head, wrapped tightly over his ears and tied beneath his chin, was a thick, filthy strip of gray canvas, stained dark with grease and old sweat.
Alex stayed frozen, his arm still locked around Mara’s waist. He didn't drop her, but he shifted his weight back onto his heels, his boots sinking into the muck as his eyes locked onto the man’s hands.
The survivor wasn't holding a spade. He was holding a four-foot length of galvanized iron fence pipe, the end of it jagged and bright with fresh orange rust where it had been sheared off.
The man didn't look at them. He couldn't see them through the heavy curtain of ferns, but his head was tilting rhythmically from side to side, his nostrils flaring as he sniffed the damp air. His face was a map of raw, purple sores, his beard matted into hard, frozen spikes of clay.
“Quiet,” Alex’s eyes screamed to Emily as she slid up beside his uninjured shoulder.
But the silt down here was treacherous. As Emily trying to stabilize her footing, the bank beneath her right heel sloughed away, sending a small cascade of frozen pebbles and gray loam sliding down into the bottom of the ditch.
Plop. Clatter.
The man in the trench didn't just turn; he exploded upward. He lunged out of the water, his bare, mud-crusted feet gripping the slippery lip of the earthwork as he hoisted the iron pipe over his right shoulder. His eyes were wide, bloodshot circles of pure, unadulterated mania, the pupils dilated so large they swallowed the iris.
"Don't speak!" the man hissed. The voice was a wet, spray-filled whisper that rattled through his teeth like gravel in a tin can. He jetted the rusted pipe toward Alex’s throat, his arms shaking with a violent, electric tremor. "Don't you do it. Don't you let them use your throat. I hear you. I hear the air moving in your chests!"
Alex didn't back down. He couldn't risk the noise of a retreat. He stood his ground, his left arm keeping Mara pinned against his hip while his right hand slowly, methodically lifted the pocket knife. He didn't open the blade yet; he just held the heavy brass casing visible, a solid piece of metal between them and the jagged iron.
Emily took a half-step sideways, her hands raised slightly, her palms open. She didn't make a sound, her lips pressed into a hard, white line, but she intentionally drew the man’s frantic gaze away from the child.
"You're the ones from the hill," the man muttered, his whisper rising in pitch, turning into an erratic, rhythmic chant. He shifted his weight from foot to foot, his toes digging into the clay. "The cabin went crunch. I heard the iron drop. I heard the girl run. You brought them down here. You brought the echoes into my ditch!"
He leaned forward, the jagged tip of the pipe coming within inches of Alex’s chest. Up close, the stench off the man was overpowering—the sharp, sour smell of a body rotting alive in baseline moisture, mixed with the bitter tang of raw hemlock bark he had been chewing to stay awake. Beneath the canvas wrap on his head, a thick, dark crust of dried mud was packed directly into his ear canals, sealing him into a self-inflicted, silent isolation.
"They're calling my sister," the man whispered, his eyes rolling toward the high canopy before snapping back to Alex. "She's been in the well for three weeks. But she keeps calling from the briars. She wants her comb. She wants her yellow dress."
He began to rock back and forth, the iron pipe dipping and rising in time with his movements. He was entirely broken, his mind reduced to a chaotic loop of survival reflexes and auditory trauma. He wasn't looking at Alex as a man; he was looking at them as acoustic lightning rods that were going to bring the sky down on his head.
"If you speak, I'll open your neck," the man snarled, his whisper dropping into a feral, guttural vibration. "I'll open it and fill it with clay. That stops the air. That keeps the woods quiet."
Mara shifted against Alex's side, her small frame giving a sudden, involuntary shudder as the freezing drizzle began to pick up again.
The man’s head snapped down, his eyes locking onto the yellow wool of her sweater. The rust-colored pipe trembled in his grip, his knuckles turning a slick, bloodless gray as he prepared to lunge forward into the brush.
The man didn't lunge. He began to vibrate, a tight, mechanical shudder that started in his bare, mud-slicked toes and rattled all the way up into the iron pipe over his shoulder. His teeth clicked together in a rapid, frantic rhythm—clack-clack-clack-clack—not from the freezing drizzle, but from a total, short-circuiting surge of adrenaline. His eyes were no longer looking at Alex; they were fixed entirely on the small, shivering ball of yellow wool tucked against Alex's hip.
"It’s too loud," the man whispered, the spit bubbling at the corner of his cracked lips. "The small one. It’s leaking. It’s leaking the air."
Alex saw the weight shift. The man’s back heel dug into the clay wall of his trench, his caked fingers locking onto the rusted fence pipe with a knuckles-white finality. He was going to swing. He was going to drive the jagged iron down into Mara’s skull just to stop the sound of her breath.
Alex didn't hesitate. He dropped his weight onto his left knee, shoving Mara violently backward into Emily's chest. The movement sent a white-hot spike of agony through his broken ribs that completely blinded his left eye, but his right hand was already moving.
His thumb caught the brass bolster of his pocket knife. With a clean, instinctual snap of his wrist, the three-inch blade flicked open, locking into place with a sharp, metallic clink.
The noise was tiny, but to the man with mud-packed ears, it was an explosion.
The rusted pipe swung down in a vicious, horizontal arc. Alex threw his torso sideways, the jagged iron edge grazing the torn canvas of his collarbone with a dry, scraping shriek. Before the man could recover his balance to swing again, Alex surged forward into the standing water of the ditch, his boots throwing up a spray of gray silt.
He didn't drive the blade into the man's throat. He didn't stab his chest. Instead, Alex used his full weight to slam the man’s right forearm flat against the rough, exposed hemlock root that formed the lip of the trench. With a single, brutal thrust, Alex drove the three-inch steel blade straight through the meat of the man’s forearm, pinning the limb directly into the frozen wood like a stake.
The man’s mouth unhinged to scream.
Alex’s left hand instantly slammed over the man’s face, his caked palms sealing the lips, his fingers digging into the mud-crusted beard to smother the noise. The man’s chest heaved violently against him, his eyes wide with a sudden, shocked realization of pain, his legs thrashing in the water of the ditch.
"Mmff! Mm—!"
Then, the high canopy answered.
Click.
It wasn't distant. It was directly above the trench line, a sharp, structural snap that vibrated through the wet pine needles like a rifle shot.
The thrashing beneath Alex froze instantly. The man’s eyes rolled back into his head, the pupils shrinking to tiny pinpricks of pure, instinctual terror as the canvas wrap around his head began to saturate with a fresh, hot sweat.
Click. Click-click.
The responses erupted from the fog to the south in a rapid, multi-directional crossfire. The entity had tracked the swing of the pipe; it had tracked the splash of the silt. It was already in the clearing, moving with that unnatural, blurring fluidness through the briars.
Alex didn't look up. He didn't pull his knife out of the root. He let go of the handle, leaving the steel buried to the brass hilt in the wood, and threw himself backward out of the ditch.
He grabbed Emily by the elbow, his fingers locking into her sleeve with a terrifying, unyielding strength.
"Run," his eyes commanded, the word unsaid but burning in his face.
Emily didn't look at the pinned man. She gathered Mara into her arms, hoisting the girl's dead weight against her collarbone with a final, desperate surge of physical leverage, and lunged off the clay bank into the thickest, darkest cluster of ferns to the north. Alex followed right on her heels, his left hand clamped over his bleeding shoulder to steady his shifting ribs, his boots tearing through the muck in absolute, terrified silence.
Behind them, in the ditch, the silence broke.
The pinned man’s survival instinct finally overrode his caution. As the first long, multi-jointed slate appendage slid through the ferns above his trench, the man let out a high, reedy shriek of pure horror—a sound that was cut short by the unmistakable, heavy thud of a seven-foot silhouette dropping into the standing water.
There was no explosion of timber, no prolonged struggle. Only the wet, tearing sound of fabric and bone fracturing under a heavy strike, followed by the rhythmic, rapid clicking of a predator mapping its meal in the dark.
Alex didn't look back. He kept his hand locked on Emily's arm, pulling her deeper into the narrow, frozen belly of the ravine, their breath rising in thin, silent plumes of gray vapor as the fog closed behind them, swallowing the trench and the screaming into the white void.
