CHAPTER 3 — "REUNION: SHARED FRACTURE"
The iron latch of the cabin door didn't just lift; it exploded upward.
Emily didn't have time to think. She scrambled backward, her sneakers skidding on the grit of the floorboards, her hip catching the edge of the heavy kitchen counter with a dull, bruising crack. Her hand instinctively snatched a rusted iron poker from beside the cold stove, lifting it by the handle, her knuckles white, her breath trapped like a stone in her throat.
The door slammed inward against the wall.
A shape filled the gray opening. Rain splattered across the threshold, driven by a sudden gust of wind that smelled of wet pine and copper. The figure was dark, hunched, a silhouette of wet canvas and broad shoulders cutting off the light from the clearing. In his right hand, a heavy, jagged piece of splintered fence post was raised, ready to swing.
"Get back," a voice rasped.
It was rough. Low in the throat.
Emily froze. The iron poker trembled in her grip, the tip hovering inches from the damp air. The room was too dark, the silhouette too sharp against the gray mist outside, but the cadence—the specific, heavy drag of the breath between the syllables—hit her chest before her mind could register the face.
The silhouette stumbled forward a step, his left hand clamped tightly over his lower ribs, his jaw tight, his eyes wild and bloodshot as they scanned the dim interior of the single room.
The gray light caught the line of his nose. The dark smear of dried crust across his knuckles.
"Alex?" Emily’s voice was barely a whisper. It felt thin, stripped of air.
The raised fence post stopped mid-air.
Alex blinked, his chest heaving under his soaked canvas jacket, his gaze locking onto her face. For one agonizing, fractional second, the silence in the cabin was so absolute she could hear the rain dripping from the hem of his cuffs onto the floor.
The wood clattered to the floorboards.
"Emily."
The distance between them vanished in a single, uncoordinated stride. The iron poker slipped from Emily’s fingers, hitting the floor with a hollow metallic clang that neither of them heard.
Then, there was only the weight of him.
His arms wrapped around her waist, pulling her into the hard, damp reality of his chest. Emily buried her face into the crook of his neck, her fingers digging fiercely through the wet fabric of his jacket, gripping the muscle of his shoulders as if the entire world outside the walls were dissolving. He was freezing. He smelled of gray mud and decayed leaves and the sharp, clean scent of the rain, but beneath it all was the familiar, unyielding heat of him.
He pressed his forehead against her temple, his breath coming in short, ragged gasps against her hair. He held her tight, his fingers locked in the wool of the oversized sweater she’d borrowed, anchoring them both to the small square of dry wood beneath their feet.
The storm tapped its indifferent rhythm against the glass behind them. Constant. Uncaring.
Slowly, the frantic racing of his heart against her palms began to even out.
Emily pulled back just enough to look down at his left side. His arm was still crooked defensively over his lower stomach, his fingers twitching slightly against the canvas. "Your ribs," she said, her voice dropping into the steady, quiet tone she used when things were breaking. "You're bracing when you breathe."
"It's fine," he muttered, though his jaw tightened automatically as she reached for the buttons of his coat.
"It isn't."
She worked the wet metal buttons free with practiced efficiency, blocking out the gray light of the window. She pushed the heavy canvas back. Underneath, his shirt was torn. She carefully slid the hem upward, her fingertips brushing the skin.
The bruise was massive. A deep, ugly blossom of black and indigo that covered the entire lower left quadrant of his rib cage. There was no open laceration, no puncture, but the swelling was uniform and thick—the blunt aftermath of an immense, localized impact.
Alex winced, a sharp hiss of air catching in his teeth, his fingers instinctively tightening on her shoulder to keep from pulling away.
"Nothing's clicking when you breathe," she murmured, her thumbs tracing the perimeter of the discoloration with feather-light pressure. "It's not punctured. But it's bad, Alex. You shouldn't be walking on this."
"I didn't have a choice." He let the shirt drop back down, his hand coming over hers, squeezing her fingers until the ache stopped.
He led her over to the low wooden dresser by the corner, his movements slow, favoring the left side. They sat down together on the floorboards, their backs pressed against the old pine drawers, their shoulders touching. The position was small. Contained. It was the only space in the last three hours that felt like it belonged to them.
"What do you remember?" Emily asked quietly, her eyes fixed on the rusted iron stove across the room.
Alex didn't answer immediately. He looked at his knuckles, tracking the dried crust of blood on his skin. "The car," he said. His voice was flat, stripping the emotion away so he could look at the pieces clearly. "We were on Route 9. Passing the old mill after the graduation ceremony. You were talking about the caps. How one of them hit the principal's shoulder."
Emily’s breath caught. "The amber clock," she whispered. "On the dash. It was 11:42."
"Yeah." Alex turned his head to look at her, his expression cast in deep, hard shadows. "11:42. Then nothing. No sound. No skid. I didn't hit the brakes, Emily. I know how a car feels when it loses traction. It didn't lose traction. The road just... ended."
"And then you woke up in the dirt."
"About a mile back. In the brush." He leaned his head back against the dresser, his eyes scanning the rafters above them. "My watch is dead. The hands are stuck at twelve. Not midnight. Just... stopped. What about you?"
"The same," Emily said. She pulled her knees up to her chest, wrapping the oversized sleeves of the wool sweater around her shins. "I woke up in the mud. Right outside. I found the cabin, and then..."
She stopped. The memory of the clearing rose up in her chest like an obstruction.
Alex felt the shift instantly. He reached out, his hand finding hers in the dark space between them, his fingers sliding into the gaps between hers. He didn't press. He just held on. "Tell me."
"There was a boy," she whispered. Her voice was trembling now, the control she had forced into herself since the door slammed beginning to fracture. "A freshman. From the school, maybe. He was hiding in the brambles by the tree line. He was so scared, Alex. He was shaking."
Alex nodded slowly. "I saw a small footprint in the clay. Angled toward the trees. And a trail."
"I opened the door," Emily went on, her eyes wide, staring at the gray glass of the window without seeing it. "I tried to call him in. I told him it was dry. He was going to come. But then... a voice called out from the woods. It was a woman. She called him Tommy. She said she was his mom. She said she found a way out."
She turned her face toward Alex, her eyes dark with a desperate, unresolved horror. "It sounded exactly like a mother, Alex. It was so warm. But it wasn't. The boy ran into the trees. He shouted for her. And then... he screamed."
The cabin was silent for three long beats of the rain.
"A real one," she said, her voice dropping lower. "A wet, terrible scream. Like he was being torn apart. But it only lasted a second. And then it was cut off. Not like it faded out—like someone turned off a switch. The whole forest went completely dead. The rain didn't make a sound. My ears were ringing. I couldn't hear myself breathe. It was a vacuum."
Alex didn't speak. His eyes left hers, tracking back to the front door he had just closed. He looked at the heavy iron latch, then down at the splintered piece of fence post lying on the floor.
His instincts tried to build a shape around the data. Warm blood trail. A child lured into the brush by a voice. An attack that leaves no body and kills the sound in the air. It didn't fit a wolf. It didn't fit a man.
"We stay inside," Alex said. The words were quiet, but they had the weight of an iron bar dropping into place. "The wood here is old, but it's thick. Whoever did that... whoever is out there, they're hunting the spaces between the trees. They're using what we expect to hear."
"You think someone's doing this?" Emily asked, her fingers tightening around his. "A person?"
"I don't know," Alex said. He shifted his weight, his ribs giving a dull, deep ache that made him darken his expression. "But a person has to touch the ground. If they try to come through that door, they have to break it. We have the advantage inside."
He reached down with his free hand, checking the pocket of his damp canvas coat. His fingers closed around the cold, heavy handle of his pocket knife. It was small—a three-inch folding blade—but the weight of the steel against his palm was a small, solid reassurance.
Emily leaned her head against his shoulder, her eyes closing as the warmth of his body finally began to penetrate the damp wool of her sweater. "We're not in the valley we know, Alex. This isn't the county line."
"I know," he murmured into the dark room.
He didn't look back at the window. He didn't want to see the trees.
But out there, past the clearing, where the gray rain was turning the black soil into grease, the sound was already moving.
Click.
It was tiny. Organic. A dry, rhythmic tapping of something dense against the wet bark of a massive pine fifty yards away. It wasn't the house settling. It wasn't the rain.
Click. Click-click.
It moved with an unnatural, architectural precision, traveling in a perfect semicircle around the perimeter of the clearing, mapping the distance between the trees and the porch, measuring the angles of the walls, learning the shape of the dry space where the two heartbeats were huddled together.
Neither of them heard it.
They had each other, and the small, rusted stove, and a gap in the world where their lives used to be.
For now, they stayed inside.
