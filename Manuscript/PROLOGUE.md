PROLOGUE

3:14 A.M.

The access road was dirt, then gravel, then dirt again where the gravel had washed out in the last rain. The van's headlights found the tree line two hundred meters ahead and the driver slowed without being told. He had done this before. They all had.

The technician in the rear cabin ran her final equipment check even though there was nothing yet to check. It was something to do with her hands. Seven subjects, secured, all sedated. The intake documentation was in order. The GPS placement schematic was loaded. The monitoring equipment would not go active until post-placement.

Protocol was simple: transport to the designated drop point, place subjects at the forest margin in the specified configuration, mark coordinates, return to the facility. The subjects would regain consciousness within two to four hours of placement, depending on body mass. What happened after that was what the program needed to know.

This was run four.

---

The soldiers got out first. The technician and Harlan pulled on their surgical masks — habit by now, not necessity — and slid the side door open. The forest smell came in immediately: iron, cold bark, and beneath both of those something she had stopped trying to name after the second run. Something mineral and close that did not belong to bark or iron or anything she had a word for.

They worked from the placement schematic. Adult male, left margin, ten meters in. Adult female, twenty-two meters. The others spaced along the grid in the configuration designed for independent awakening response — the study required individual data, not group behavior. The subjects were moved from the gurneys to the forest floor without incident. All of them breathing correctly. All of them presenting normal sedation vital signs.

The last one was the girl.

She was smaller than the intake documentation had led the technician to expect, or she looked smaller in the dark. She wore a yellow school sweater and small sneakers and she was breathing with the slow, even depth of deep sedation. The technician carried her herself — the way you carry a sleeping child, the girl's head against her shoulder, because putting her on the gurney for thirty meters seemed like more procedure than the situation required.

Placement marker three: the base of a large pine, thirty meters into the tree line, eastern margin. She set the girl down on her side the way the protocol specified, arranged her arms, checked the airway. Penlight close to the lips — good color, breath misting faintly in the cold.

She stood up.

She did not look back.

---

The van was moving at 3:41 A.M. when the voice came from the tree line to the right.

A man's voice. Clear and close — close enough that the driver heard it over the engine without the window down. *Help me. Please. Someone help me.* The specific register of someone badly hurt, breath behind the words, the consonants wet and effortful, the kind of voice that cuts through trained response because the training says: possible subject, early wake-up, retrieve.

The driver slowed.

The soldier in the passenger seat — Castellano, three runs, never said much — had his hand on the door before the van stopped. The other soldier, Reyes, leaned between the seats. "Could be a subject," Reyes said. "Early wake-up. It happens."

"Protocol is retrieve," Harlan said from the rear.

Castellano got out.

She watched through the windshield as he walked toward the tree line, flashlight sweeping the road's shoulder, his other hand resting on the sidearm at his hip. He had been on two prior runs and had never heard anything at the tree line on the drive out. This was the first time.

He was fifteen meters from the tree line when the voice said his name.

His first name. Not his rank, not his last name — his first name, the name on no document connected to this program, the name that lived only in the mouths of people who knew him. The voice that said it was not the same voice that had said *help me*. It was a different voice entirely, and it said his name the way someone says a name when they need you to come to them.

Castellano stopped walking.

His flashlight hit the ground. Still on, the beam pointed sideways across the gravel, illuminating nothing useful.

Reyes was out of the van before anyone said anything. Rifle up, tactical light cutting into the dark between the trees, sweeping for Castellano. "Castellano," he said, once, at low volume. The beam found the flashlight on the ground. It found the gravel. It found the edge of the tree line.

It found nothing else.

Reyes took four steps toward the tree line.

Then Reyes was gone.

Not gone the way a person walks away. Gone the way a light goes out — present, then not. One frame to the next. The tactical light went with him, its beam tilting upward as it went and then cutting off entirely, and the road was dark except for the van's headlights and Castellano's flashlight still burning sideways on the gravel pointing at nothing.

Nobody in the van moved for four seconds.

The technician heard herself say something. She did not know what she said. Harlan had his hand on her arm and was already telling the driver to go, go now, just go — and the driver was pulling a hard turn on the narrow road, the rear wheels dropping into the soft shoulder and spinning before the tires caught and the van lurched forward and accelerated.

They drove for eleven seconds.

Then something landed on the roof.

The impact was not like a branch, not like debris, not like anything that had fallen. It was deliberate weight, distributed, the roof panel bowing inward three centimeters under the load of something that had come from above and intended to stay. The overhead lights flickered. Harlan said a word. The technician grabbed the equipment rack with both hands and looked up at the ceiling of the van as the metal above them spoke in low, sustained tones she felt in her back teeth.

More weight arrived.

Not from above. From the sides — the panel windows going dark in a way that meant the darkness outside them had closed to within inches of the glass. The driver made a sound she had never heard a person make before and she realized she was making the same sound and could not stop.

The radio was still on. A weather report from a station two hundred miles east. A woman's voice, calm, listing temperature and wind and the chance of rain.

The van stopped moving.

---

The flashlight on the access road burned for a long time after that.

It went out sometime before morning, when the battery finally gave.

The forest did not make a sound.
