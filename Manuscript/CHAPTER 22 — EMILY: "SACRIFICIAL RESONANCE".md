CHAPTER 22 — EMILY: "SACRIFICIAL RESONANCE"

The stairwell was twelve steps up.

She had gone down them once, walking against every instinct, holding Mara's hand and following Alex's back into the sealed lower air of the cave. Now she went up them in the opposite direction and she felt, at the seventh step, the seep resume against the bind — not a sharp thing, not pain exactly, just warmth spreading along her left shoulder blade and into the fabric, the wound deciding the elevation was enough to ask for more. She didn't stop. She put her hand on the back of Mara's collar and moved her through the stairwell door first and followed her through it and let it close.

The booth was as they had left it.

The thick plexiglass was still intact — every fracture still theoretical, every weakness still potential. The console screen was still displaying the system boot data, the same white text on black, the scrolling hardware check done and finished, the system settled into its waiting state: DUAL OVERRIDE REQUIRED. TERMINAL A STANDBY.

She crossed to the console.

Her name for the switch, once she found it, was *ugly* — a thick-shafted toggle inside a recessed slot, designed to be gripped and held rather than flipped, the engineering of something meant to endure pressure and not come loose. She put her right hand around it without engaging it. Felt the resistance. Measured it against what four minutes and twelve seconds of continuous grip would ask of her shoulder, the forearm Ben had cut, the specific inventory of what was left in her hands right now. She did not have good information about that last question. She had been answering it wrong for the last several hours and finding out later she had been wrong.

She let go of the switch and looked through the glass.

---

The cave was fifty-five feet below the booth floor, and the pack was in it.

They were not where they had been when Alex had led her and Mara back up the stairwell. The positions had changed — not dramatically, not a rout, but the quality of the movement had shifted in the way she had learned to read in the last two months of watching things move through the dark. Patrol was a particular kind of easy circulation, shapes cutting slow arcs through a known space, the body language of organisms with nothing to locate. What she was looking at now was not that. The shapes below moved with a directional interest. Not toward the booth specifically. Toward the generator sound, which was coming up through the floor of the booth in a low sustained vibration she could feel in the balls of her feet, and toward something else.

She was not naive about what the something else was.

Her left hand lifted unconsciously toward her shoulder blade. She stopped it. The bind was there — she could feel the warmth spreading in it — and confirming what she could already deduce did not help anything.

She looked for the fail-safe room.

It took her forty seconds to find it through the glass, which was forty seconds longer than she'd expected. The cave floor was dark in the way deep limestone caves are dark — absolute, textured only by the booth's inadequate emergency panel throwing light partway into the space before giving up. The fail-safe room was built into the far cave wall at floor level, sixty meters out and below. She found it not by seeing it but by finding the sliver of light that the room's interior threw out through its viewport — a thin wire-mesh rectangle, barely the size of a piece of paper at this distance, backlit from inside.

Alex was in there. He had to be in there; there was nowhere else for him to be.

She stared at the sliver of light until she was certain she could find it again quickly, then she looked away and checked Mara.

Mara was at the left wall, not the back wall. She had placed herself with her shoulder blades against the concrete, her feet planted, her hands at her sides. She was watching Emily with the particular steadiness of a child who understood exactly what was about to happen and had decided that watching it was better than not watching it. Emily crossed to her and crouched until she was eye level and held Mara's face in both hands for a moment — just that, both palms, the warmth of the girl's cheeks, the unbroken dark of her eyes — and then she pointed to the back wall. All the way to the back. She pointed twice and looked at Mara until Mara nodded.

Mara went to the back wall.

Emily stood up and went to the console.

---

The generator hum had been building for minutes. She hadn't registered the increments until she realized the vibration in the floor was stronger than it had been when she entered the booth — not stronger in a dramatic way, but detectable, the kind of change you register in the soles of your feet before your conscious mind catches up. She looked through the glass.

The pack had reoriented.

Not all of it — the shapes at the far end of the cave floor were still in their own circulation, still reading as that slow, familiar frequency. But the nearer shapes, the three or four presences that had been closest to the stairwell door even during the first crossing, had changed their orientation in a way she could now read clearly. They were moving with their faces toward the booth. Toward her. The generator was below and the generator was loud and the generator was a reason to move toward the structure that housed it, and she understood in the same instant that the generator was not the only reason.

She put her right hand on the upper-switch grip.

She looked at the viewport across the cave. The sliver of light was still there.

She waited.

The glass was thick — she did not know the exact specification but the booth had been built for exactly this kind of proximity to exactly these kinds of organisms, and the plexiglass reflected that. The first indication that it was not impervious came not from a sound but from a physical transmission through the booth's floor: a deep, irregular percussion, coming up through the concrete supports, the booth's frame conducting what was happening below it. Something was making contact with the structure.

She kept her hand on the switch.

The percussion continued. Not frantic — deliberate. She had seen the creatures test environments before. The door of the root nest, the grate in the floor corridor. They found the physics of a thing by touching it, by returning pressure and listening to what the material said back. Whatever was below the booth platform was finding out what the platform was made of.

She looked at the plexiglass.

The first fracture appeared in the lower left corner — not a crack exactly, a whitening, a region of stress that the glass reported the way glass reports stress: quietly, without drama, a line no thicker than a hair running from the lower left corner toward the center and stopping. She watched it for a moment. The percussion below had not stopped.

Then the second contact came and she felt it in her knees.

---

From the back wall, Mara said nothing. She watched.

Emily stood at the console and kept her right hand on the switch and looked through the glass at the shapes that were now, unmistakably, ascending. She could not see the booth's structural supports from inside the booth — the plexiglass looked out into the cave and the supports were below her sight line — but she could read their progress in the way the percussion was changing: closer, higher, the vibration in the floor shifting from a deep structural report to something more specific, more present, the feeling of weight on the booth walls themselves rather than the platform below.

The shapes she could see from above were moving toward the booth. Not the slow patrol frequency — something faster, more directed. The generator's sound had been in the cave for long enough. The blood had been in the air for long enough.

She looked at the viewport.

The sliver of light was unchanged.

*Come on*, she thought, and then she stopped thinking it, because it was not useful, and because she could not know what he was managing on the other side of the cave and she did not have Alex's talent for containing the full arithmetic of a situation inside something that looked, from the outside, like patience.

The glass issued a sound — not loud, a high, thin creak that traveled from the lower left corner toward the upper right. Not the previous stress fracture. A new one, longer, the glass expressing what was being asked of it. She watched the line extend and stop.

The percussion below her was no longer structural. It was immediate.

She counted what she had: her right hand on the switch. The warmth spreading steadily down her left side through the bind. The forearm that would ache in earnest if she held the grip for the full duration. The knowledge that Mara was at the back wall and the back wall was eight feet from the glass.

She looked at the viewport.

The light changed.

It was a small change — the quality of the light in the sliver shifted, the way the quality of light shifts when a body moves through it and the shadows reorganize. She could not see him clearly. She could not see him at all, really — only the evidence of his presence in that small rectangle of dim light across sixty meters of dark cave. But the light changed in a specific way: purposeful, directed, a movement toward the console rather than through the room.

He was at the switch.

She engaged Terminal A.

The console screen updated in the same instant: TERMINAL A: ENGAGED. AWAITING TERMINAL B. The words were white on black, very clean, the unambiguous report of a system that had been waiting years for exactly this. She kept her grip. One second. Two seconds. She watched the screen and she watched the viewport and she kept her right hand on the switch with the full understanding that if she let go the whole thing reset.

TERMINAL B: ENGAGED.

The screen changed: DUAL OVERRIDE CONFIRMED. CHARGE INITIATING. CHARGE CYCLE: 4:12.

The vault door sealing was not quiet. The steel door at the base of the stairwell — twelve steps below her, the door she had come back up through — gave a mechanical report she heard through the concrete: a deep compression, a lock engaging, the sound of something very heavy becoming immovable. The lower sector was sealed. The stairwell door was sealed. The lower sector held the pack. The lower sector held Alex.

The timer was running.

The glass issued a sound she had not heard before.

---

Not a creak — a report. The difference between a material reporting stress and a material reporting damage. The lower left fracture had propagated: a network now, spreading from the corner in three directions, the branches thin but real, the plexiglass communicating that the question of its integrity was no longer theoretical but in progress. She could see, through the fracture network, the shapes outside the glass. Not below the glass now. At the glass. The booth's structural supports had been enough to reach it.

The shape at the lower-left corner was not describable except in terms of what its contact with the glass was doing to the glass. Pressure, distributed and specific. The high-mounted emergency panel above the booth console threw inadequate light and she could see the fracture network growing as the pressure increased and she could not see through it well enough to read what was outside it and so she read what the glass told her instead.

She thought about Alex in the room below the cave floor, in the four-by-six space, his left hand against his ribs, right hand on the lower switch, the viewport in his front wall looking out into the lower sector where the vault doors had just sealed. The stairwell door at the base was sealed. He could not come up. She could not go down.

4:12 from dual engage.

She let go of the console edge with her left hand.

She stepped away from the console, toward the glass.

---

The wound side first — she turned until her left shoulder was toward the glass. She could feel the seep through the bind, a warmth that had become continuous in the last ten minutes, and she understood that whatever was in the air in this booth was what had oriented them to the glass before she ever engaged the switch. She was already the signal. Had been since they re-entered the building in Ch. 18. There was no keeping that information from the cave.

What she could do was concentrate it.

She moved until she was four feet from the glass.

The fractures were at eye level from here — she could see the network clearly, the way the lines spread and crossed and built on each other, the plexiglass going from transparent to a frosted lace of stress. Through it, in fragments, she could read the shape of what was on the other side: tall, close, its physical reality communicating itself through the glass in pressure and heat differential. More than one. The percussion in the floor had not stopped. They were still ascending. Still finding the booth.

She hit the metal frame with her right fist.

The sound was dull, industrial, her knuckles against the steel rail that framed the observation window, and it went into the cave through the glass and the damaged plexiglass amplified it, the stress fractures conducting the vibration differently than intact glass would have. She hit it again. And again. The pain in her hand was immediate and she kept going and she heard herself make a sound — not a planned sound, not a tactical one, just the sound a person makes when they have been saving their voice for three days in a place where sound was death and they are now deliberately spending all of it at once.

She shouted.

The glass shook.

The shape on the other side responded. She could not see the response clearly through the fracture network but she could feel it in the rail under her right hand — the transmission of what was outside pressing against what was inside, the glass conducting that meeting. The fracture network in the lower left spread to the lower right. A third network began forming above the midline, moving toward the upper-center.

She hit the frame again. She kept shouting. She kept the wound side toward the glass.

Behind her, at the back wall, she could feel that Mara was there — not by sight, not by sound, but in the specific spatial awareness she had developed for Mara over the last weeks, the way she always knew where the girl was without looking. She did not look. She kept her face toward the glass and her right fist on the metal frame and she made as much noise as her body would produce.

The timer was somewhere in the three-minute range.

She did not know exactly. She had not looked at the console since she stepped away from it and she was not going to look at it now. She had the switch engaged. The system was running. The timer was a fact that existed independent of her attention.

The glass fractured toward the center.

---

What she would remember later — in the way she would remember everything about the facility, in compressed and partial images, the way trauma keeps its files — was not the sound or the pain but the arithmetic of it. She had run the numbers in the moment she stepped toward the glass. Four feet. The plexiglass was thick but it was damaged. Three-plus minutes was a long time for damaged plexiglass under sustained pressure. She had run those numbers and had stepped forward anyway.

The arithmetic had not said she was likely to survive the full charge cycle standing where she was standing. It had said she was more likely to survive it than Alex was, if the pack found a way out of the lower sector and into the fail-safe room. The arithmetic had been, in that sense, very clear.

She hit the frame.

The glass issued a sound she felt in her teeth.

Behind her, Mara had not moved.

She hit the frame again and the fracture network in the upper center of the glass spread to meet the fracture network in the lower center and for one long moment the plexiglass was holding all of it — every network, every line, every accumulated insult of the last several minutes — holding it the way a thing holds everything right before it cannot hold anymore.

She kept her shoulder toward the glass.

She kept her right fist on the frame.

She kept making noise.

The glass let go.
